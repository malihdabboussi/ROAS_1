Every incentive system is a behavioral prediction. When the prediction is wrong — when people optimize for the metric instead of the goal — the system produces outcomes that are not just unhelpful but actively harmful. This page synthesizes the frameworks for understanding and preventing perverse incentives.

**Core insight:** Incentive structures that tie rewards to proxy metrics invite gaming. The more measurable the metric, the easier it is to optimize for the number without producing the underlying result. Good incentive design anticipates how the incentive will be gamed, not just how it will be followed.

## The Cobra Effect

The Cobra Effect occurs when an attempted solution to a problem makes the problem worse as a direct result of unintended consequences or perverse incentives.

**The origin story:** In colonial India, the British government offered a bounty for every dead cobra to reduce Delhi's dangerous snake population. Citizens responded rationally: they bred cobras specifically to collect the bounty. When the government scrapped the program after discovering the scheme, breeders released their now-worthless snakes into the city. The cobra population ended up significantly higher than before the program began.

The government measured the right thing (dead cobras) but did not account for how rational actors would respond to the incentive. The metric decoupled from the goal the moment it became rewarded.

## Perverse Incentives

A perverse incentive is a reward structure that yields an unintended and undesirable result contrary to the interests of the incentive makers. This typically occurs when a reward is tied to a specific proxy metric rather than the underlying goal, encouraging people to optimize for the metric in ways that undermine the actual purpose.

Perverse incentives are structurally predictable. They emerge whenever:
- A metric is treated as equivalent to the goal it was designed to represent
- Individuals can separate their behavior toward the metric from their behavior toward the goal
- The reward for hitting the metric is significant enough to motivate behavioral adaptation

Common business examples: sales teams that hit quota by over-promising and under-delivering; content operations that hit view count targets by optimizing for clickbait over retention; customer success teams that close tickets quickly without resolving the underlying issue.

## Premortems for Incentive Design

The premortem technique from decision-quality-premortems.md applies directly to incentive design. Before launching any incentive structure, conduct a premortem specifically focused on behavioral adaptation:

- "How might people game this incentive?"
- "What is the most destructive way someone could achieve this metric?"
- "If someone wanted to hit this number without producing the actual outcome, how would they do it?"

Anticipating these adaptations allows for the design of safeguards or alternative reward structures before the system is deployed. The behavioral premortem is the primary tool for preventing the Cobra Effect.

This is the inverse of the Success-Planning Imbalance: most incentive designers only model compliant behavior (how honest actors will respond). Robust incentive design models adversarial behavior (how someone would game the system at maximum strategic effort).

## Designing for the Goal, Not the Metric

The structural solution to perverse incentives is to tie rewards as closely as possible to the actual goal rather than to a proxy:

- **Revenue over activity:** Commission on closed revenue rather than calls made. The metric (calls) can be gamed by dialing wrong numbers; the goal (revenue) cannot be faked.
- **Customer outcomes over satisfaction scores:** Retention and repeat purchase over NPS surveys. Surveys can be timed; revenue patterns cannot be easily manipulated.
- **Multi-metric systems:** No single metric captures the full goal. Combining metrics that are harder to jointly game produces more robust incentive structures.

Where direct goal measurement is impossible, the next best approach is to combine multiple proxy metrics that are inversely correlated in gaming scenarios — making it difficult to optimize all simultaneously without actually producing the desired behavior.

## The Broader Pattern

The Cobra Effect is one instance of a larger pattern: complex systems produce unexpected consequences when participants optimize for measurable outputs at the expense of systemic health. This appears in:

- **Goodhart's Law:** 'When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.'
- **Teaching to the test:** Educational systems that reward standardized test scores see teachers optimize for test performance rather than learning.
- **Vanity metrics in marketing:** Follower count, impressions, and open rates that can be inflated without corresponding business impact.

Vibey's content strategy frameworks implicitly address this: the 30-day content measurement window prevents single viral posts from distorting format decisions (a form of incentive manipulation). The niche-to-broad ratio resists the temptation to optimize for view counts at the expense of conversion quality.

**Key takeaways:**
- Reward the goal, not the proxy. The more measurable the metric, the more gameable it is.
- Run a behavioral premortem before launching any incentive system: "How would someone game this?"
- The Cobra Effect is the canonical reminder that rational individuals respond to incentives, not intentions.
- Goodhart's Law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops measuring what it was designed to measure.
- Multi-metric systems are harder to game jointly than single-metric systems.
- Perverse incentives are structurally predictable — they are not failure of character, but of system design.
