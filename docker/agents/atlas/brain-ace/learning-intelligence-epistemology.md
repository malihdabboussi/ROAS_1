Most people confuse having information with possessing intelligence. The gap between the two is experience — and closing that gap requires a different relationship with the concepts we're taught. This page synthesizes the frameworks governing how real intelligence is developed and how self-imposed limitations prevent it from being used.

**Core insight:** Education systems declare information as knowledge before the experience step. This produces people who can recall but not reason — who know what to think but not how to test whether what they think is true.

## The Intelligence Flywheel

Intelligence is not a fixed capacity — it is a compounding cycle with three stages:

1. **Information** — raw external data encountered through reading, observation, and instruction. The starting material. Most formal education stops here.
2. **Knowledge** — information that has been internalized, processed, and connected to existing understanding. This is where meaning is made. You cannot have knowledge without having thought.
3. **Experience** — testing knowledge through real-world action and observing what happens. Experience validates, refines, or overturns knowledge. It is the only step that produces genuine competence.

The flywheel compounds: experience generates new information (from results, feedback, and unexpected outcomes), which gets processed into deeper knowledge, which is tested through further action. Each cycle produces a more sophisticated practitioner than the last.

**The Education-Intelligence Gap** is what happens when the cycle is broken at the first step. Traditional education systems declare information as knowledge without requiring the experience step. Students are conditioned to rely on recall — retrieving what was told to them — rather than testing or questioning whether it holds in the real world. The result is a confusion between holding information and possessing intelligence. Someone can know that X is true without understanding why, and therefore cannot adjust when X fails to produce the expected result.

The failure mode this creates: people who know many things, can articulate frameworks fluently, but freeze or make poor decisions when reality doesn't match the information they were given. Information without experience is a map with no terrain correspondence.

## The Fact vs. Truth Distinction

Critical thinking operates on a fundamental distinction most formal education doesn't teach:

- **Facts** are objective — they exist independent of perspective and are, in principle, verifiable.
- **Truths** are subjective — they are the interpretations, narratives, and meanings individuals attach to facts based on their context, culture, and experience.

There is often one fact, but many versions of truth. Critical thinking requires questioning the 'truth' one has been taught in order to discover the underlying fact. The person who received a 'truth' as a child (about money, identity, capability, or risk) and never interrogated it is operating on a narrative they didn't choose.

The practical discipline: when encountering a belief about what is possible or impossible, ask: Is this a fact (grounded in physics, evidence, or measurement) or a truth (an interpretation I inherited from someone else's experience)?

## The Shed Metaphor: Deconstructing Self-Imposed Limitations

Most people live within invisible boundaries — 'sheds' — that were drawn around them by external influences: parents, teachers, peers, societal norms. The shed defines what feels safe, appropriate, and possible. Initially, these boundaries are external. Over time, they are internalized and experienced as permanent rules about the world.

**The Three Layers of Constraint Internalization** explain why these limitations persist even when the person consciously wants to break through them:

1. **Factual Grounding** — constraints feel real because they are linked to tangible challenges. Money is scarce. Bureaucracy exists. Time is limited. The concrete difficulty makes the boundary feel like a wall rather than a choice.
2. **Social Discomfort** — stepping outside the boundary feels isolating because others remain within theirs. The person who challenges an assumed limitation often does so alone, which reads as evidence that the limitation is real.
3. **Moral Conditioning** — boundaries are often framed as virtues. Being 'realistic,' 'humble,' or 'grounded' becomes a moral identity. Challenging the constraint feels like a character flaw, not a strategic choice.

All three layers compound. The person who wants to break through a self-imposed limitation must simultaneously deal with a real practical challenge, social isolation from those who remain inside the boundary, and the internal voice suggesting that ambition beyond the shed is arrogance.

**The Shed Metaphor's core observation:** The perceived value of a challenge consistently exceeds its actual difficulty. The anticipation of what it will take to step outside the boundary is almost always worse than the actual experience of doing it. The shed is larger than its perceived dimensions.

**The Constraint Validation Technique** is the operational check: before accepting a limitation as a deal-breaker, ask:

*'Is this a real constraint, or is this a circle someone else drew around me that I have accepted as permanent?'*

This separates physical or logistical impossibilities from internalized social or psychological restrictions. Real constraints are stubborn regardless of perspective. Shed constraints dissolve when the question is asked directly.

This technique is the psychological complement to the First Principles Constraint Analysis in decision-quality-premortems.md — both ask the same question from different angles: what physical law actually prevents this? If there is none, the constraint is a choice.

## Connecting the Frameworks

The Intelligence Flywheel and the Shed Metaphor are related at a deep level. Formal education produces information without experience — and leaves people with inherited 'truths' they never tested. Untested truths become the walls of the shed. The Fact vs. Truth Distinction is the cognitive tool for identifying which beliefs are factual (and therefore load-bearing) and which are inherited truths (and therefore candidates for questioning).

The full chain: break through the shed by testing inherited truths against facts, running experience cycles through the Intelligence Flywheel, and building genuine intelligence rather than accumulated information.

**Key takeaways:**
- The Intelligence Flywheel requires all three stages: Information, Knowledge, Experience. Most education stops at stage one.
- The Education-Intelligence Gap is structural: recall-based education produces people who know without being able to adjust when knowledge fails.
- There is one fact, but many truths. Critical thinking is the act of interrogating the truths you were given to find the underlying facts.
- Shed constraints operate through three layers: factual grounding, social discomfort, and moral conditioning. All three must be recognized to move through them.
- The Constraint Validation Technique: 'Is this real or a circle someone else drew?' Physical constraints are load-bearing. Inherited truths are not.
- The anticipated difficulty of breaking through a self-imposed limitation consistently exceeds the actual difficulty. The shed looks bigger from inside than it is.
