# Consumer Product Love and Trust Wedges

## Context

A new cluster of entries centers on early-stage consumer products where the decisive signal is not broad approval, but intense trust and behavioral love from a narrow user segment. The strongest case study is Groww, which used transparency, education, and near-zero onboarding friction to convert first-time Indian investors.

## Core Insight

Indifference is the worst product signal. Early products should polarize around a painful problem, create visible user love through retention and word-of-mouth, and postpone monetization when revenue would contaminate trust or value validation.

## The Story

The first principle is to avoid the **Indifference Trap**. A neutral 'meh' reaction produces no retention, referrals, or learning. Love validates the core value proposition; hate clarifies who the product is not for. Consensus products become bland because they optimize for moderate approval instead of passionate need.

This leads to the **Build for Passion, Not Consensus** framework: choose a narrow group that desperately needs the solution and make them irrationally happy before broadening the market. True PMF shows up behaviorally through frequent use, high retention, willingness to pay, and unprompted word-of-mouth, not compliments.

Groww is the canonical wedge. The company operated for roughly four years with zero revenue, using a free, transparent, zero-commission model to remove monetization as an unknown and build trust first. In opaque industries like finance, radical transparency becomes the product, not a compliance checkbox: present all choices, fees, and tradeoffs with an e-commerce mental model.

For zero-to-one users, education and friction removal are part of the product. Replace jargon with plain language, explain concepts visually, and map every click from install to first success. Groww automated hidden compliance steps like KYC, photo matching, and signature recognition so first-time investors could complete a first SIP in minutes.

Direct user communities complete the loop. Put the first 50-100 users in WhatsApp or Telegram groups, watch confusion live, and treat repeated questions as usability evidence. Many apparent feature requests are actually transparency gaps: users ask for X because they cannot see why the current workflow behaves as it does.

## Key Takeaways

- Optimize for love or hate, not neutral consensus.
- Isolate one major unknown at a time; if possible, make monetization the last unknown after trust and PMF are proven.
- For first-time users, education and friction removal are product requirements, not marketing extras.
- In opaque categories, radical transparency is a competitive moat.
- Early direct-user communities are live usability labs; repeated confusion beats survey feedback.
